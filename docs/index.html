Single-trial cortical prediction of the H‑reflex

The question

Does the brain change as the cord learns?

Rats are rewarded for making the soleus H-reflex larger or smaller. Over weeks the spinal cord itself changes — a 20% shift is the laboratory's criterion for success. Boulay, Chen and Wolpaw (2015) showed that cortical activity influences reflex size. What was not known is whether that relationship is altered by learning, and whether it can be seen on a single trial.

How it works

From a 2006 database to a trained model

01

Recover the archive

MyISAM tables from the Elizan III system, loaded into a local MySQL server.

02

Decode the trials

Little-endian 16-bit samples, two channels: soleus EMG and cortical ECoG.

03

Measure the physiology

M-wave, H-reflex and pre-stimulus background, all background-subtracted.

04

Learn a representation

An autoencoder compresses each cortical trace to 48 numbers, using no labels.

05

Test, with controls

Freeze the encoder, then ask what it predicts — against five separate nulls.

One thing worth flagging

The archived decoder documents these files as big-endian. They are not — decoded that way the signal is noise. Reconstructing the format correctly was the step that made everything downstream possible.

What we found

Three results, including one that says no

Every number below is corrected for stimulus intensity and motoneuron-pool excitability — the two things that move the reflex without any learning involved.

Reproduced

Conditioning works, both directions

Corrected H-reflex amplitude rises in up-conditioned animals and falls in down-conditioned ones. Three of five clear the 20% criterion in the trained direction.

log₂ fold change −1.55 to +1.71

Positive

Cortex predicts the reflex, trial by trial

Decoders trained and tested within five-day blocks — so slow recording drift cancels — beat a shuffle of their own block's labels in 99 of 101 blocks.

R² = 0.037  ·  t(4) = 3.81, p = 0.019

Negative

Average-state drift is not learning

The intuitive analysis looks convincing and fails three separate controls. It tracks slow change in the recording, not the animal.

sham drifts in 5 of 6 animals

In proportion

What actually explains reflex size

Cortex and stimulus share variance, because the stimulus drives both the cortical evoked response and the reflex. Only the non-overlapping part counts as a cortical contribution.

Why the headline number went down, not up

An earlier estimate put the cortical share far higher. That version removed the stimulus across all trials at once and then measured cortex block by block, which leaves between-block stimulus variance behind — and cortex tracks the stimulus. Removing it inside each block gives 0.037. It is the smaller claim, and the one that survives.

Open

What we are working on next

Animals that learned more show a larger increase in cortical coupling (r = +0.71, n = 5). With five animals that is a direction, not a result — the remaining recordings would settle it. We are also pursuing an idea of Dr. Carp's: the experimenter's reward criterion, adjusted daily as the animal succeeds or struggles, is an independent record of how hard the task was for that rat. It may describe engagement with the task in a way the reflex alone does not.
